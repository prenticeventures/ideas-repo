The opportunity in one page

WHIP is worth a small, focused test. Its most interesting feature is that an individual can earn a discretionary $10,000 to $30,000 for original information that helps produce criminal charges. You do not need to bring a lawsuit, wait for a recovery or use a lawyer for a named submission. DOJ expressly allows original analysis of public data.

That is a different opportunity from building and financing a False Claims Act case. An analyst can give investigators a specific lead and a reproducible explanation of what to examine. Investigators can then obtain evidence the analyst cannot access. The analyst still needs to contribute something new and useful, and the information must ultimately help produce charges.

The business remains unproven. The program launched five days before this research cut-off. No completed WHIP payment was independently verified. The written rules leave important questions about existing investigations and professional sources of information.

Business question Current answer
Could you qualify as an outside healthcare expert? Potentially, as an individual using your own qualifying analysis. Expertise alone does not establish originality.
Can your company receive the award? No. Entities are excluded. The rules do not settle arrangements between a founder and a company that funds the work.
Do you need a lawyer? No for a named tip. Yes to submit anonymously, and DOJ must verify identity before payment.
How much could you earn? DOJ anticipates $10,000 to $30,000 per distinct qualifying tip. The amount and decision to pay are discretionary.
What triggers payment? Normally an unsealed indictment, information or criminal complaint resulting from the tip. A conviction or collected recovery is not required.
Is there a minimum fraud amount? No minimum loss or forfeiture. Even an unsuccessful scheme can be considered if it produces qualifying charges.
Does a high billing rank qualify? Not by itself. The analysis must reveal new information, materially add to agency knowledge and help produce charges.
Is payment quick? DOJ intends to decide promptly after qualifying charges. It gives no dependable submission-to-charge timetable.

Sources: DOJ governing terms and FAQ, especially questions 7, 10, 12 and 13, both updated October 20, 2026.

What the program covers

Healthcare coverage is broad. It includes federal healthcare offenses involving health benefit programs and fraud against patients or others in healthcare. It can reach private health insurance, rather than only Medicare and Medicaid. The federal definition of a health benefit program includes qualifying public and private plans. A commercial insurance dispute still needs facts supporting a covered crime. 18 U.S.C. §24(b).

For fraud involving state, local or tribal benefit programs, the terms also recognize schemes where some loss ultimately falls on a federal program. Do not assume the first payer named in a transaction determines the entire funding chain.

DOJ highlights four priorities:

Priority Public information that can support screening What remains to investigate
Hospice CMS provider utilization, payment, quality and ownership records. Eligibility, actual care, documentation, referral arrangements and knowing misconduct.
Home health CMS service and payment summaries, enrollment and ownership information. Whether care occurred, patient eligibility, certifications, referrals and actual service dates.
Special services, including autism care Validated Medicaid summaries, licensing, enrollment and enforcement records where available. Actual attendance, service delivery, diagnosis, inducements and payer-specific requirements.
Day care Public licensing, subsidy audits and existing government reports. Actual attendance, eligibility and claims. This is a stated program priority, but outside the current healthcare experiment.

The data types above come from the project's data audit. They are screening inputs, not a tested WHIP submission strategy. The existing national Medicaid file still needs validation before it can support payment rankings.

Atherectomy is not on the short priority list, but the broader healthcare scope can cover a qualifying case. The saved Tabaie prosecution shows that government analytics can identify an outlier. It does not establish an outside tip, a WHIP award or a new opportunity to report that already-public case. DOJ case announcement.

What an analyst needs to contribute

DOJ distinguishes a new analytical finding from a new presentation of old information. Public data may be the input. The useful output must reveal information that was not generally known and was not already known to DOJ.

For this business, a useful packet should answer five questions:

  1. Who is involved? Resolve the individual, organization, NPI, facility and relevant dates.
  2. What specific conduct needs investigation? Explain more than the provider's position in a ranking.
  3. What did your work newly reveal? Separate facts already in the sources from the inference your method adds.
  4. What are the strongest lawful explanations? Show which have been checked and what remains unknown.
  5. What could investigators check next? Identify the records or witnesses that could confirm or reject the concern.

This is a preparation standard for the proposed business, not an additional DOJ filing rule. The reporter does not have to finish the government's investigation or prove guilt before reporting. But a generic spreadsheet of high-volume providers gives DOJ much less to work with than a precise finding, source trail and investigation roadmap.

Output What it establishes WHIP implication
A sorted list of high-volume providers Published billing differs across providers. A starting point. Originality, misconduct and usefulness remain unestablished.
A reproducible comparison tied to independent dated records and a specific payment requirement A defined inconsistency worth testing, if identity, dates and rule applicability survive review. More useful, but DOJ may already know it. A clerical error or permitted exception could still explain it.
A new factual finding with corroboration and a clear path to confirming criminal conduct A more actionable lead. Better aligned with the written criteria. Payment still requires charges and DOJ approval.

The completed 5,730-row experiment tested the first stage. It did not test original evidence production or WHIP eligibility. Requested government data could add service dates or claim details, but the data holder must permit the intended use. WHIP does not grant access to protected claims or override a data-use agreement.

Eligibility questions that matter for your background

Your healthcare experience is an analytical advantage. The source of information matters separately.

Situation Business consequence
Your own analysis of public records Expressly contemplated by the rules, provided it reveals qualifying original information.
Information learned through compliance, audit, accounting or internal-investigation work Potentially excluded. Some exceptions exist, but the rules have overlapping provisions that need clarification.
Information connected to legal representation or privilege Separate restrictions apply. Hiring an attorney does not make otherwise excluded information eligible.
Information supplied by someone whose source is excluded Passing it to an outside analyst does not automatically cure the exclusion.
Existing company duties, management authority or participation in misconduct Can affect eligibility or the award. Do not treat ordinary employment and participation in a scheme as the same thing.

Healthcare employment is not a blanket disqualification. Before relying on professional knowledge, record how it was obtained, the role involved and any applicable restrictions. Using independent public analysis avoids some professional-source issues, but does not guarantee eligibility. The rules reference identifies the precise provisions and exceptions.

Two material questions in DOJ's text

Question Why it matters
Can a new tip qualify when the government already has an investigation? The originality rule expressly contemplates new information in an existing matter. The voluntary-submission rule also requires the absence of a government investigation. The FAQ's employer-subpoena example adds further tension. The sources do not explain how DOJ reconciles this wording.
Which professional-source exceptions survive the broader exclusion? One provision allows exceptions for certain executive and compliance information, including specified urgent harms or a 120-day internal-reporting route. Another separately excludes information from audit, accounting, compliance and internal investigations without spelling out the same exceptions.

These questions do not establish that the program is unusable. They do mean we cannot confidently classify every lead from the published criteria. Unknown government knowledge or an undisclosed investigation cannot be resolved with a public-record search.

How submission works in practice

Use the official America.gov/fraud entry point if a submission is eventually authorized. DOJ says it may deny a reward solely because a tip did not use the established portal. An ordinary agency hotline, a report to an insurer and an FCA lawsuit are different routes.

The research inspected the live first intake step without entering tip facts, personal details or files:

Observed feature Practical implication
Narrative limited to 2,000 characters Prepare a short factual summary that states what happened, what is new and how you know.
Up to five attachments, 50 MB each Curate a small evidence package. Do not rely on sending eighteen separate source files.
PDF, CSV, TXT and JSON are among accepted types The existing analysis can be summarized in supported formats. Python, HTML and ZIP were not on the displayed accept list.

A practical package could use three attachments: a short evidence memo, a focused CSV of relevant rows, and a TXT or JSON source-and-method record. State what the evidence cannot establish. Describe authorized investigators' next checks. These are recommendations for clarity, not an official DOJ template.

The portal's privacy notices describe government sharing for review, investigation and related purposes. Treat entering report content as an external disclosure. The review stopped before entering content or advancing beyond the first step. The submission-route reference separates observed controls from later-stage unknowns.

What happens after a tip

The program is more than a one-time upload. DOJ can ask for records, interviews, testimony and proactive work under law-enforcement supervision. It may never contact the reporter. There is no published service deadline that lets a founder plan cash receipts.

The anticipated sequence is submission, agency review, investigation, qualifying public charges, award review and a determination. Earlier investigator contact is not an award. Charges are not an automatic promise to pay. The program says its final award decisions are discretionary and cannot be appealed through the program or judicially reviewed.

DOJ may also bar frivolous, fraudulent or obstructive submissions. Its examples include three or more such submissions, and intentional material falsehoods can carry separate consequences. This is not a rule that three good-faith tips that fail to produce charges automatically lead to a ban. The terms describe the quality and conduct problems, not merely unsuccessful outcomes.

Funding, first-award claims and payment history

DOJ announced a $25 million Treasury commitment for implementation. That establishes announced backing, not a public record that the entire amount has been transferred, reserved for tip payments or distributed. The underlying reward authority is 28 U.S.C. §530C(b)(1)(L). Its general authority is broader than WHIP's stated award range and does not enlarge WHIP's published promise.

Business economics and the smallest next test

WHIP could reduce the litigation burden of earning a reward, but it does not remove the cost of finding new evidence or the time spent cooperating.

Illustrative cost per lead Required share paid at a $20,000 award
20 hours at $50 plus $500 cash = $1,500 7.5%
50 hours at $50 plus $500 cash = $3,000 15%
100 hours at $50 plus $500 cash = $5,500 27.5%

These are break-even requirements before tax and delay, not observed probabilities. Include failed leads, source checks, preparation and follow-up time. Do not count an award per provider, charge or defendant: DOJ decides whether tips are distinct.

A second program may remain available later, but WHIP does not guarantee stacked awards. CWA has its own duplicate-payment rules. An FCA case has separate filing, disclosure and eligibility requirements. Choose the intended route before external reporting rather than assuming one upload preserves all options.

Apply a WHIP review to the existing evidence test

Hypothesis: the first evidence packet from the report's proposed public-record test can communicate a specific, new and reproducible finding within WHIP's format and identify its remaining eligibility gaps.

Exact change: add a WHIP fit review to that first packet. Write a 2,000-character summary and organize up to three supported attachments. Identify the new fact, criminal theory, lawful alternatives, source of information and what investigators should check. Do not start another volume screen.

Keep fixed: the existing snapshots, procedure definitions, provider selection and evidence standards. The known Tabaie prosecution can illustrate the format, but cannot count as a new candidate or qualifying tip.

Cost and measurement: at most two additional founder hours and $0 cash. Record whether the packet has a clearly stated new finding, reproducible support, a credible investigation question, source eligibility and an explicit list of unknowns. This measures readiness for further review, not the chance of payment.

Stop rule: stop after two hours or when the finding remains only a billing rank or a recycled allegation. Do not purchase data or submit a tip to rescue a weak packet. An unclear professional source or unresolved eligibility issue stays unknown.

Detailed rules and unresolved wording

DOJ WHIP rules: source check and business implications

Research cutoff: October 25, 2026. DOJ published the current WHIP terms and FAQ on October 20, 2026. I opened both pages and the terms' linked endnotes. No source-access problem prevented this review. This is a source summary, not legal advice.

Main use

WHIP is a discretionary individual reward for original information that leads to an unsealed criminal charge. It is not a civil False Claims Act share or a payment for a useful tip by itself. A solo public-data analyst is not categorically excluded, but must contribute a new, nonpublic insight that is not already known to DOJ and that materially advances the matter. The timing and voluntary-submission rules create a significant uncertainty when the government is already investigating related conduct.

Requirements and practical effect

Rule Current DOJ terms Practical consequence
Covered subject Federal health care offenses and related crimes involving health benefit programs, or fraud against patients or others in healthcare. Other named federal benefit programs also qualify. DOJ prioritizes hospice, home health care, day care, and special-services fraud. Terms say an initially state, local, or tribal scheme can qualify if some loss ultimately falls on a federal program. A private-insurance or provider dispute is not automatically covered. The facts still need to fit the criminal subject definition and lead to a qualifying charge. See Terms, “Subject Area,” item 6 and Endnote 3.
Who may receive payment A person must be an individual. A company or other entity cannot receive the reward. Exclusions also cover DOJ or law-enforcement officials, employees and contractors, certain family or household members, elected or appointed foreign officials, meaningful participants in the reported crime, and people who knowingly mislead or obstruct DOJ. DOJ may still consider someone whose role was minimal and who was among the least culpable, but that decision is discretionary. A business may not claim the award in its own name. The terms do not exclude ordinary company employees solely because they are employees, but their source of information and reporting duties can matter. The family exclusion differs slightly between the terms and FAQ. The terms refer to an “other close relative” of a DOJ employee or contractor, while the FAQ refers to listed close relatives of an official, employee, or contractor. The extent of this exclusion is unclear. See Terms, “Tipster Reward Eligibility,” exclusions 1 to 6 and FAQ, Q14 and Q17.
Insider and professional-source exclusions Information obtained through attorney-client privileged communications, paid legal representation, audit or accounting work, compliance or internal investigations, or certain corporate reporting duties is generally excluded. Exceptions may apply for imminent patient harm or other listed urgent harms, obstruction of an investigation, or after 120 days following specified internal reporting in some cases. Do not assume an employee, consultant, lawyer, accountant, auditor, compliance officer, investigator, or person receiving information from one of them can use it. The precise source and role matter. Under “Original Information,” item 4(4), exceptions apply to some executive and compliance sources. A separate item 4(6) also excludes information from an audit, accounting, or compliance relationship or internal investigation without stating an exception. How these provisions work together is unclear.
Original information and public data Independent analysis may examine public information, but it must reveal something not generally known or available. The information provided must itself be nonpublic, previously unknown to DOJ, and materially add to what DOJ has if DOJ already has related information. Information entirely contained in court or agency proceedings, government reports, audits or investigations, or news is excluded unless the tipster was the source. Public documents can be inputs, but merely collecting, summarizing, or repeating them is not enough. The useful output must be a genuinely new insight. DOJ may already know it even if the source material is public or the investigation is not public. See Terms, “Original Information,” items 1 to 4 and FAQ, Q10.
Voluntary timing A submission must precede a related request, inquiry, or demand directed to the tipster or their representative by DOJ, federal law enforcement, or a civil enforcement agency. It cannot be owed under a specified agreement related to a criminal or civil enforcement matter. The terms also require the absence of a government investigation or a threat of imminent disclosure to the government or public. FAQ Q9 gives examples of obligations that make a report involuntary. The “absence of any government investigation” wording is broader than the separate originality rule, which says that information can be original even if DOJ has already opened an investigation. DOJ does not explain how it reconciles these provisions. FAQ Q9 says an employee may still submit voluntarily when the employer received a subpoena if the employee is not personally obligated to provide information. The FAQ example does not explain how that fits with the broad investigation wording. This is a material uncertainty for leads found after an investigation starts. See Terms, “Voluntary,” items 1 and 2 and FAQ, Q9.
Truthful, complete, and cooperative The tipster must give all information they know about the misconduct, their own role, and matters DOJ may ask about. They must cooperate in related criminal or civil actions, including interviews, grand jury or trial testimony, producing records, and, if requested, proactive work under law-enforcement supervision. The obligation can require substantial time and continuing participation after the original report. Concealing the tipster's own role can defeat eligibility. See Terms, “Truthful and Complete,” item 4 and “Cooperation,” item 5.
Charge and causal link The tip must be specific, credible, and timely enough to cause DOJ to open or reopen an investigation, or examine different conduct in an existing investigation, and the information must lead to criminal charges. A charge means an indictment, information, or criminal complaint obtained and unsealed by DOJ. A tip that prompts only a civil recovery, policy change, or internal company investigation does not meet this WHIP trigger. It is not enough that a charge later concerns the same company. See Terms, “Leading to Criminal Charges,” items 1 to 3 and FAQ, Q8.
Amount and timing DOJ anticipates $10,000 to $30,000 for each distinct tip. There is no minimum loss or forfeiture. No reward is payable while charges remain sealed or otherwise nonpublic unless DOJ chooses to pay sooner. More than one person may be rewarded if each independently supplies qualifying information. The range is not guaranteed. DOJ alone decides eligibility, amount, and whether tips are distinct. The trigger is an unsealed charge, not a recovery, but the tipster does not control when charges are filed. See Terms, “Leading to Criminal Charges,” items 2 and 4, “Additional Considerations for Payment,” items 1 and 2, and FAQ, Q7, Q12, and Q13.
Management and culpability factors A person with a minimal role may qualify if DOJ decides the person is among the least culpable. The amount may reflect that role. DOJ may also deny a reward based on management responsibility, compliance failures, company culture, or failure to act on known red flags. A nonparticipant outsider is the clearest fit on this dimension. The terms do not promise that a manager who did not directly commit the crime will qualify. See Terms, “Additional Considerations for Payment,” items 2 and 3.
Same information and multiple programs DOJ says the reward may be received at an early stage while the person remains eligible to seek a later award under another government program, including CWA. FAQ says multiple people need separate original, nonpublic information. Identical information from a later tipster is not original. WHIP does not promise that two programs will pay on the same conduct. Current CWA guidance says eligibility for another program is no longer an automatic bar, but CWA will not issue duplicative awards and may reduce or deny an award when the person has received or may receive another award for overlapping information. The WHIP terms do not resolve whether an FCA relator may also receive a WHIP award. The FCA is a separate civil lawsuit under 31 U.S.C. § 3730. A WHIP tip does not itself file an FCA case or satisfy its separate filing steps. Do not count overlapping payments as additive without a program-specific determination. See Terms, Introduction and “Leading to Criminal Charges,” item 4, FAQ Q16, and CWA Guidance, “Eligibility for Submission,” item 1(b), footnote 3, and “Additional Considerations for Payment,” item 3(b)(v).
Submission channel Terms require a completed, signed intake form through the DOJ portal. DOJ may consider a report sent another way, but only in its discretion if it reaches decision-makers in time. FAQ says DOJ may deny an award solely because a tip did not use the established portal. The portal is effectively the only reliable submission route under current guidance. A report to an unrelated public tip line may not preserve WHIP eligibility. See Terms, “Procedures for Submission,” item 1 and FAQ, Q2 and Q3.
Lawyer and identity A named submission does not require a lawyer. An anonymous report must be submitted by an attorney, who verifies identity and certifies accuracy. DOJ must know and verify identity before paying. DOJ may require identity disclosure earlier. Counsel can submit anonymously, but DOJ can require identity disclosure and must verify identity before paying. Attorney fees may be needed for anonymity, but the terms do not set or reimburse them. See Terms, “Additional Guidance for Public Awareness,” items 2 and 3, “Procedures for Submission,” item 2 and FAQ, Q17 and Q19.
Confidentiality and retaliation DOJ says it will not publicly disclose identifying information except when required by law or DOJ policy, or when disclosure to a defendant is required in a proceeding. It may share information with other enforcement agencies for law-enforcement or public-protection reasons. Retaliation may affect a company’s cooperation credit or trigger enforcement action, but the terms do not promise personal protection or compensation. A tipster should not treat identity as absolutely protected or the program as a standalone anti-retaliation remedy. See Terms, “Additional Guidance for Public Awareness,” items 1 and 4.
Award decision and pilot duration All award determinations are discretionary, cannot be appealed, and are not subject to judicial review. The terms confer no enforceable rights. The pilot has an initial three-year term. Meeting stated criteria does not create a right to payment. Unlike the CWA guidance, the WHIP terms do not explain what happens to pending submissions when the three-year term ends. A reported $10,000 launch check was announced, but no DOJ award determination or completed payment was independently verified by this cutoff. See Terms, Introduction, “Procedures for Award Determinations,” item 3, Endnote 1, FAQ Q13, and CWA Guidance, “Introduction,” footnote 2.
Lawfully obtained evidence and bad submissions The terms exclude information obtained through a method that violates federal or state criminal law. DOJ states that submitting unlawfully obtained information does not protect the person from prosecution for obtaining it. DOJ may permanently bar frivolous or fraudulent repeat submissions, including after three or more such submissions, and materially false statements may carry criminal penalties. Do not use unauthorized access or submit speculative or duplicate claims. See Terms, “Original Information,” item 4(5), “Additional Guidance for Public Awareness,” item 6 and Endnote 2.

What the current program summary should emphasize

The existing program overview correctly identifies individual eligibility, the public-data-analysis possibility, the $10,000 to $30,000 range, lack of a minimum loss, the charge-stage trigger, attorney use for anonymous tips, and discretion. The following details materially change how a solo researcher should assess the route:

  1. Public sources are allowed as inputs, but DOJ requires the submitted information to be a nonpublic insight unknown to DOJ. A public-data report that merely reproduces public allegations fails this standard.
  2. The terms make voluntariness depend on there being no government investigation or threat of imminent disclosure. That rule appears to conflict with the separate statement that originality is assessed regardless of whether DOJ already opened an investigation. DOJ has not explained the tension.
  3. The FAQ says an employee may still report voluntarily when their employer receives a subpoena, if the employee is not personally obligated to respond. The FAQ does not explain how this example fits the terms' broad requirement that no government investigation be underway.
  4. The source-of-information exclusions are extensive. Certain corporate officers, compliance staff, auditors, accountants, lawyers, internal investigators, and people working from their excluded information may not qualify. Limited exceptions exist, including imminent patient harm, obstruction, and a 120-day internal-reporting route for specified insiders. The terms also have a separate, broader exclusion for information from audit, accounting, or compliance work or an internal investigation. They do not say whether the earlier exceptions apply to that separate exclusion.
  5. The family exclusion differs between the terms and FAQ. The terms exclude an “other close relative” of a DOJ employee or contractor, while the FAQ lists spouse, parent, child, or sibling of an official, employee, or contractor. The difference in wording leaves the scope uncertain.
  6. Cooperation can mean testimony, document production, and proactive work under law-enforcement supervision. This is a continuing commitment, not just a tip submission.
  7. The portal is not merely preferred. DOJ says it may deny eligibility solely because the tip came through another channel.
  8. Multiple awards are not guaranteed. WHIP says a person may still be eligible for another program, while CWA bars duplicative payments and may reduce or deny an award for overlap. WHIP does not state the FCA interaction.

Privacy, notices, and certifications

The first step offered a button for a Paperwork Reduction Act notice, not a consent checkbox. The notice identified OMB control number 1505-0284, an expiration date of November 30, 2026, and a 30-minute estimated burden. It says giving information is voluntary and that submitted information may be shared with appropriate federal agencies, Inspectors General, law-enforcement organizations, or other authorized entities for review, referral, investigation, audit, enforcement, recovery, litigation, or other authorized government purposes.

The first step displayed no certification checkbox or signature field. The DOJ WHIP terms nevertheless say a qualifying tip must be voluntary, truthful, complete, original, and in writing, and describe the submission route as a completed and signed Intake Form. The form screen where any signature or certification is collected was not reached, so the signing mechanism and exact certification wording remain unknown.

The landing page says a name is optional, but WHIP rules qualify that statement. DOJ says a person submitting anonymously must be represented by an attorney, whose submission must certify that the attorney verified the reporter’s identity and reviewed accuracy and completeness with them. A person submitting on their own must provide enough information for DOJ to contact and verify identity. Before any award is paid, the person must disclose identity and DOJ must verify it. The Department may share information with another enforcement agency for a law-enforcement or public-protection purpose. Its confidentiality promise is limited: it will not publicly disclose information reasonably expected to reveal identity except as required by law or policy, or when disclosure to a defendant is required in a proceeding.

Evidence and use rules that affect an analyst

DOJ terms require original information derived from independent knowledge or independent analysis, non-public and not already known to DOJ, and materially additive to what DOJ already has. Independent analysis may examine public data, but the analysis must reveal information not generally known or available. A public-data report that only repeats a public allegation, published court claim, government report, or news story is not original under the terms unless the reporter was the source. A researcher should document the analytical steps, what the public sources show, what new inference the analysis supports, and what independent corroboration is available.

The reporter must act voluntarily, provide all material facts including their own role, and cooperate with DOJ. The terms exclude information obtained through specified internal compliance, audit, investigation, client-representation, or privileged channels, subject to stated exceptions. They also warn that the reward program does not protect someone from prosecution for unlawfully obtaining information. The form’s first screen does not explain these eligibility requirements, so a narrative that is technically accepted by the portal is not necessarily reward-eligible.

WHIP: launch rules and public track record

Checked October 25, 2026. This note separates the Justice Department's new Criminal Tip Rewards Pilot Program, called WHIP in its launch announcement, from existing False Claims Act settlements and from rewards discussed at the launch. It is based on government records published by this date. It does not establish that no confidential payment has been made.

What is publicly confirmed so far

DOJ announced WHIP on 20 October 2026. As of 25 October, I found no DOJ award determination, DOJ payment announcement, court filing, or other primary record confirming a WHIP payment to a tipster. DOJ's terms say it will protect information that could identify a tipster, subject to legal and policy exceptions. Public silence therefore cannot rule out a confidential award.

The Associated Press reported a planned $10,000 launch check for Nick Shirley but said it was not presented in front of journalists. That does not establish whether payment occurred privately.

The official White House launch pages confirm the program announcement, but do not document the payment. No DOJ intake record or Reward Determination for this example was located. The public record does not explain how the announcement fits the written criteria. Treat it as an announced example, not a verified award or completed payment.

Other DOJ enforcement announcements after launch are not WHIP payment evidence. For example, the DOJ's 22 October health care fraud takedown announced charges in multiple cases. It did not name a WHIP tipster or say any charges resulted from a WHIP tip. A charge, indictment, guilty plea, civil settlement, forfeiture, or False Claims Act relator share is not itself a WHIP award.

What the program offers and requires

DOJ's 20 October terms establish an initial three-year pilot. A person may qualify for a discretionary award of $10,000 to $30,000 when truthful, complete, original information, submitted voluntarily in writing, leads to public criminal charges in a covered area. “Charges” means an unsealed indictment, information, or criminal complaint. DOJ says the payment is intended for around the charging stage, but the terms require the Department to review the claim and issue a Reward Determination. DOJ may deny an award even when charges are filed. That determination is not appealable and is not subject to judicial review.

The individual must cooperate with the investigation and related proceedings. A company cannot be the tipster. A person can submit without a lawyer, but anonymous submissions must go through an attorney. DOJ must verify the person's identity before paying an award. The terms also exclude certain insiders, law-enforcement personnel and relatives, people who participated in or knowingly profited from the reported crime, illegal acquisition of information, and information obtained through privileged legal work or compliance and internal audit duties, subject to stated exceptions.

The program currently covers federal health care offenses and related crimes involving health care programs, patients, or others in the health care industry. It also covers fraud on benefits administered by USDA, Labor, HHS, HUD, Education, VA, the Department of War, and SBA. DOJ highlights hospice, home health care, day care, and special-services fraud, including autism services. Covered conduct may include kickbacks, medically unnecessary care, services not delivered as billed, or services not delivered at all. The terms say some state, local, or tribal program fraud can qualify if a federal program ultimately bears some loss.

For outside data work, the opening is narrow but real: an individual may submit their own analysis of public records. The analysis must reveal nonpublic information that is not generally known, was not already known to DOJ, and materially adds to the government's information. A high billing rank by itself is not established as sufficient. In a case already under investigation, an analyst would need new and material information. The public materials do not spell out how DOJ will assess a public-data-only analysis in practice.

Funding and legal authority

DOJ says the Treasury Department is committing $25 million to implement the program. This is an announced program-level commitment, not a per-tip amount or an award already made. The statute allows executive agencies to provide DOJ with reward funds, but I found no public transfer, obligation, or spending record showing that Treasury's announced amount has been transferred or paid out.

DOJ cites 28 U.S.C. § 530C(b)(1)(L), which authorizes rewards advertised to assist DOJ, under procedures set by the Attorney General. The law generally caps an individual reward at $3 million. A reward of $250,000 or more needs personal approval from the Attorney General or President and written notice to designated congressional committee leaders within 30 days. WHIP's stated $10,000 to $30,000 range is below those thresholds. The statute also says a decision not to pay, or the amount approved, cannot be judicially reviewed.

The legal source is an authority for reward payments. It does not itself guarantee that an individual qualifies or that the announced $25 million has been made available to DOJ. The DOJ terms say the program creates no enforceable rights.

Directive 26-12 and enforcement focus

Assistant Attorney General Colin McDonald issued Directive 26-12 on 1 October 2026, nineteen days before WHIP's launch. The directive tells the Fraud Division to use criminal and corporate-enforcement tools against health care, government, tax, and trade fraud. It emphasizes voluntary disclosure, cooperation, remediation, corporate accountability, and recovering proceeds. DOJ's 20 October release describes WHIP as a response to a gap in existing whistleblower programs, which often pay only after criminal proceedings, forfeiture, and appeals are complete.

For healthcare, DOJ's written terms and launch release both name hospice, home health care, day care, and special services. The release adds examples including autism care. That is a priority list, not evidence that those subjects will receive more awards or that every case in those categories is covered. The formal rules still require a qualifying federal offense or covered federal program connection, original information, a voluntary written tip, criminal charges, cooperation, and DOJ approval.

What does not count as a WHIP track record

DOJ's public enforcement page for the 22 October Los Angeles health-care takedown says data analysis identified Harold Tabaie as an extreme outlier in Medicare atherectomy billing. The indictment followed. The government source does not credit an outside analyst or whistleblower. It does not describe this as a WHIP tip or disclose a reward. It is an example of government use of data analysis in fraud detection, not evidence that public analysis generated a paid tip.

Likewise, False Claims Act settlements and relator shares are a separate civil process. A relator share announced by DOJ, a settlement amount, or a guilty plea does not establish a WHIP payment. The project's separate reward examples and litigation review distinguishes announced relator shares from verified receipts. I found no primary source proving that any WHIP award had been paid by 25 October 2026.

Open questions for an outside analyst

  • The launch message said WHIP would pay for tips leading to charges, and President Trump publicly described a 1% commission. DOJ's written terms instead set a discretionary $10,000 to $30,000 range and require review after a qualifying charge. The written terms control the published program criteria, but DOJ has not explained the difference in a public FAQ.
  • The rules allow independent analysis of public data but require the resulting information to be nonpublic, not already known to DOJ, and materially additive. The examples do not say what level of statistical analysis meets that threshold.
  • DOJ provides a submission form, eligibility criteria, and a discretionary review process. No submission-to-payment deadline, working status-tracking process or public award ledger was verified. The portal promises a reference number, but the research did not submit a report or test later steps. Its confidentiality policy may limit disclosure of individual awards.
  • No primary record reviewed identifies a completed WHIP award, its tip, the date of payment, or whether the award was paid from Treasury's announced commitment. The launch-stage record is too short to estimate award frequency, timing, or likely income.
