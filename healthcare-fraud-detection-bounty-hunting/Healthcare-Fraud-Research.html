Overview

There is a real opportunity to investigate healthcare fraud using data. There are also several ways to get paid for successful work. The unanswered business question is whether a solo operator can repeatedly turn a suspicious pattern into evidence that earns enough to cover unsuccessful investigations and long payment delays.

Public Medicare data are a useful starting point. Our experiment found a surgeon charged in a major atherectomy case among the national top twenty providers by selected procedure volume in each of three years. That shows an outsider could have seen the billing pattern. It does not show that the outsider could prove the procedures were unnecessary or earn a reward.

This report evaluates the opportunity for an experienced healthcare professional working alone with AI. It covers Medicare, Medicaid, private insurance and other federal health programs. The data strategy includes free downloads, government records available by request and controlled government research datasets. A data purchase costing less than $10,000 is an option if it would answer a specific question and the intended use is permitted. No purchase or outside request has been made.

What the research supports

Question Answer
Can an outsider get paid? Yes. Federal and state False Claims Act cases, DOJ reward programs and some state Medicaid informant laws provide payment routes. An ordinary fraud hotline usually does not.
Can analysis of public data qualify? Sometimes. DOJ's new WHIP program expressly allows original public-data analysis. Lawsuit-based routes have additional requirements. Repeating a public allegation or producing an outlier list is not enough.
Is there useful accessible data? Yes. Medicare billing, prescribing, provider identity, ownership, exclusions and other records support screening. Government applications and records requests may add useful detail, but access and permitted use must be checked.
What is the hardest step? Showing what makes specific claims false, rather than merely unusual. Clinical records, actual service dates, referral arrangements or payer records may be needed.
Does healthcare expertise help? Yes. It can improve code selection, clinical interpretation and the assessment of reasonable explanations. It does not by itself provide access to patient records or permission to reuse restricted data.
Is this ready to support dependable income? No. A specialized investigation business is conditionally viable, but its cost per useful lead, payment rate and cash timing remain unproven.

The report separates three stages. Screening finds something worth examining. Corroboration establishes facts that support a particular violation and tests reasonable explanations. Payment requires the outcome specified by the chosen program.

The case studies describe pending criminal charges, not findings of guilt. The financial examples distinguish claims submitted, amounts paid, recoveries and reporter awards. Those numbers are not interchangeable.

Detailed program terms, the 50-state plus D.C. index, data inventories and source records are linked throughout. The main report focuses on business decisions. The references preserve the legal and technical detail needed for further diligence.

WHIP deserves a separate look

Read the WHIP deep dive. It examines public-data eligibility, professional-source restrictions, the live intake format, funding and the first-award reporting. It also identifies unresolved wording on tips involving an existing government investigation. A named tip does not require a lawsuit or lawyer, but a qualifying charge and DOJ approval are still needed for payment.

What matters most for your business

  • FCA cases offer large potential shares, but require litigation and collection. The private plaintiff is called a relator. Attorney fees, case costs and who funds the work must be agreed. The statute does not supply upfront funding.
  • WHIP is the clearest express fit for original public-data analysis. DOJ launched it on October 20, 2026, so there is no mature history for estimating success rates or payment delays. Charges are the normal milestone, not tip submission. DOJ announcement and FAQ.
  • The corporate pilot pays after forfeiture, meaning the government has taken qualifying money or property connected to the crime. Alleged losses and large billed amounts are not its payment base.
  • The Medicare reward's $1,000 ceiling is too small to support substantial investigation costs by itself.
  • Selling analysis to an insurer or attorney would be a separate service business. No buyer, contract or willingness to pay was tested here.

Can public-data analysis qualify?

Yes, under some conditions. WHIP expressly recognizes original analysis. For an FCA case, the question is whether your analysis reveals an important fact that was not already disclosed and supports specific false claims.

The public-disclosure rule can block a case that repeats substantially the same allegations already in certain public sources. An original-source exception can sometimes preserve it. These rules are separate from whether the underlying evidence is strong. A novel ranking can still fail to show fraud.

The practical test is straightforward: what does the analysis establish beyond “this provider bills more than peers,” and why is a lawful explanation less convincing? Courts have rejected cases where statistical differences remained consistent with proper coding. Federal rule, court examples.

3. Government data: downloads, requests and approved access

The data strategy should not stop at what can be downloaded today. There are three useful levels: open data for screening, government records or aggregates available by request, and controlled claims data available through an approved project. Each can add different evidence.

The existing 19-source inventory records fields, identifiers, years, access routes and test status. The data audit contains the detailed limitations. The government access assessment evaluates request-based routes, costs and permitted use. Documentation-verified access is not the same as an approved application or a tested delivery.

An additional provider-pair data review verifies that the free CMS and NBER shared-patient files for 2009 through 2015 remain accessible. They contain provider pairs and aggregate counts without patient identifiers. Newer DocGraph files are listed separately under commercial and noncommercial licenses. The historical files do not overlap the 2021 through 2023 billing experiment, and patient sharing does not establish an actual referral or misconduct.

Can the approved project produce provider-specific evidence that may be referred to enforcement or an attorney in connection with a potential reward?

Choosing data for a specific fraud theory

A dataset is valuable when it changes what we can conclude. More rows alone do not accomplish that.

Investigation question Minimum useful additional data What would still be missing
Is a provider an unusual procedure outlier? Provider, code, period, setting, services and a defensible peer group. Why the care was provided and whether claims were false. Public summaries already address much of this screen.
Are repeat procedures or combinations unusual? Stable patient key, service dates, codes and relevant diagnoses. Chart-level necessity, laterality detail where absent, actual treatment and intent.
Are services implausible because of licensing or exclusion dates? Correct provider identity, service dates, historical status and the applicable payment rule. Exceptions, attribution and whether the identified payment was actually prohibited. Annual totals may be too coarse.
Are referrals or ownership arrangements suspicious? Reliable entity relationships, referral or ordering links, payment records and dates. The actual agreement, services and intent. Ownership or industry payments alone do not establish misconduct.
Did a substance-treatment network bill for care that was not delivered? Payer claims plus attendance, treatment records, staffing or other service evidence. Public business and licensing records cannot establish what happened to each patient.

Your healthcare experience is most useful in defining the right comparisons and testing these explanations. AI can accelerate extraction and reconciliation, but it cannot supply missing observations.

4. What the two saved fraud cases demonstrate

Both principal prosecutions were pending at the October 25, 2026 cut-off. The defendants are presumed innocent. The full case studies and source index preserve the court identifiers, chronology and record details.

Atherectomy: Harold Tabaie and Hany Malek

The indictment alleges that a Southern California clinic network billed Medicare for unnecessary vascular procedures, used patient inducements and submitted some claims under other physicians' names. It describes a scheme from January 2016 through August 2023. The defendants were charged in October 2026. The alleged totals are approximately $134,955,000 submitted and at least $54,817,000 paid. These are charging-document figures, not final losses or recoveries. Indictment.

DOJ expressly says data analytics identified Tabaie as a national atherectomy billing outlier. That makes this a strong example for testing public-data screening. It does not identify the government's exact dataset or full investigative method. DOJ local announcement.

An outsider could link Tabaie's identity to NPI 1346286663 and compare his procedure volume with peers in CMS's public files. Our experiment did that. The 2023 data were public by May 2025, before the indictment, although we did not establish when investigators first found the case or whether these exact data versions were available then.

The missing evidence is decisive. Public aggregates do not show imaging, indications, operative notes, actual rendering clinician, referral discussions or patient inducements. The allegation that claims used other doctors' names also means an individual-NPI screen can miss part of a network.

Business lesson: the initial screen is feasible and cheap. Building a case about unnecessary care requires a second source of evidence. No reviewed record documents a reporter reward in this case.

Substance-treatment network: Nathan Young and related defendants

The principal indictment charges Nathan Young, David Young, Honey Phipps and Cody Phipps in a network involving dozens of facilities. It alleges patient brokering, inducements, false enrollment and billing for unnecessary, deficient or unprovided care from May 2020 through May 2026. It identifies private insurers including Anthem, Aetna, Cigna and UnitedHealthcare. The alleged totals are $1,619,276,243 submitted and at least $210,274,793 paid. Public copy of indictment.

Those figures are not a $1.6 billion recovery or a proven $210 million loss. A related guilty plea by Nicholas Moore concerns his own case and does not establish the guilt of the four principal defendants. The federal prosecution's origin is not identified in the reviewed sources. DOJ names investigating agencies, but that does not tell us who first supplied the lead.

Some concerns were public earlier. A Beverly Hills nuisance case dates to 2020, and Aetna brought a civil fraud action in 2023 with a later amended complaint. Reporting also described an ongoing federal investigation before the indictment. Those records could help map entities and allegations. They do not establish that public records caused the investigation or that repeating them would produce a new, payable tip. The case reference identifies these records and their limits.

An outsider lacks a Medicare-style public claims ledger for these commercial insurers. Testing the core allegations would require evidence about enrollment, attendance, treatment, broker payments and insurer claims. A government all-payer database might improve utilization visibility if access and use were approved. It would not, by itself, reveal whether therapy actually occurred.

Business lesson: public records can reveal a network worth investigating, but the main evidence gap is wider than in the atherectomy screen. No reporter award was identified in the reviewed records.

What the result establishes

A public-data screen can identify a meaningful billing outlier and can be reproduced at very low retrieval cost. Tabaie appeared in the national top twenty in all three years under the fixed method.

It does not estimate fraud-detection accuracy. We chose the case after enforcement made it known, which creates hindsight bias. The current snapshots may differ from earlier releases. A CMS bulletin confirms 2023 data were public by May 22, 2025, but not that these exact versions were public before the alleged scheme ended or before investigators began work. Full-year 2023 also includes months outside the alleged conduct window.

Reproducing an already-public prosecution does not create an original lead. The next test must examine a different uncertainty: whether additional records can support a specific violation in a new case.

7. Business feasibility and economics

Assessment: conditionally viable as specialized investigation

The opportunity is credible enough to justify a small next test. It is not yet supported as dependable income from automated tips.

The strongest model combines healthcare judgment, careful data work and investigation. Public data find candidates. Requested records or approved claims access may answer narrower questions. The work earns value when it explains why particular conduct is inconsistent with a payment rule and survives reasonable clinical and operational explanations.

Your healthcare background is an advantage at that interpretation stage. The expanded data budget also creates options beyond public summaries. Neither is a reason to buy a broad dataset before checking whether it contains the missing evidence and permits the proposed use.

Main requirements File a detailed lawsuit under seal, give the government the evidence, and identify specific false claims. Public-disclosure and first-to-file rules may block the case.

Further details

The FCA lets a private person file a lawsuit on the United States' behalf. This type of case is called a qui tam case. The relator is the person who brings it. The law does not require the relator to work for the company being sued, and its definition of “person” can include an organization. The complaint and supporting evidence are filed under seal, which means the defendant is not notified at first. The government gets at least 60 days to review the case and often asks for more time. DOJ may take over, decline, or join the case later.

There is no payment just for sending a tip. The government or relator must recover money. The normal share is 15 to 25% if DOJ proceeds, and 25 to 30% if DOJ declines and the relator wins. A separate statutory rule allows a share of no more than 10% when the court finds an intervened case is based mainly on specified public disclosures other than information the relator provided. That 10% rule is separate from the public-disclosure bar and its original-source exception. See 31 U.S.C. § 3730, especially subsections (b), (d), (e)(4), and (f).

The public-disclosure bar can block a case when substantially the same allegations or transactions already appeared in a federal hearing, a congressional or GAO report, another federal report or investigation, or news media. Unless DOJ opposes dismissal, the court must dismiss a barred case unless DOJ brings it or the relator qualifies as an original source.

The law has two original-source paths. A person may qualify by voluntarily giving the information to the government before it becomes public. Someone who learned the information independently may also qualify by adding an important fact to public allegations and voluntarily giving that information to DOJ before filing. A public-data researcher should identify the new fact their method uncovered, not just say they are skilled at analyzing records. The complaint must also describe the fraud in detail and identify false claims. Outlying billing statistics alone may not show that any claim was false.

If the case succeeds, a relator may recover necessary expenses and reasonable attorney fees and costs from the defendant. The government does not advance case expenses. A relator who litigates after DOJ declines can face the defendant's fees if the case was clearly frivolous, vexatious, or brought mainly to harass. A lawyer is required to pursue the case for the United States. In the Ninth Circuit, Stoner held that a nonlawyer cannot represent the United States in a qui tam case pro se. See Stoner v. Santa Clara County Office of Education.

The House Office of the Law Revision Counsel preliminary version of §3730 used here says it contains laws in effect on September 10, 2026. That is the latest official date checked, not a guarantee that no later amendment has occurred.

What the public-data cases show

  • Integra and Providence: Integra was not affiliated with Providence and relied mainly on its statistical analysis of CMS claims data. It did not have insider medical records. The Ninth Circuit issued an unpublished, nonprecedential decision in 2021. It found Integra had not plausibly shown the claims were false because Providence might simply have been better at compliant documentation and coding. It reversed the denial of dismissal and sent the case back. The panel did not decide whether online news media counted as a public disclosure. Ninth Circuit memorandum.
  • Integra and Baylor Scott & White: The Fifth Circuit affirmed dismissal of an upcoding case based on public Medicare data and statistical comparisons. The data were also consistent with lawful explanations, and the complaint lacked concrete facts showing why the claims were false. Statistical outliers alone did not establish falsity. Fifth Circuit opinion.
  • Silbersher and Allergan: A patent lawyer used public patent records and other government records to argue that patents obtained through fraud delayed generic competition and raised public drug costs. The Ninth Circuit said patent prosecution could count as a federal hearing for the public-disclosure bar, then sent the case back. It did not decide whether Silbersher was an original source. On remand, the district court found he was not an original source, and the Ninth Circuit affirmed in an unpublished 2025 decision. 2022 published opinion, 2025 disposition.
  • Silbersher and Valeant: The Ninth Circuit reversed dismissal because the public records did not themselves disclose the key fact he assembled: the defendants took conflicting positions in patent proceedings. This is a narrow example of a public-record analysis adding a missing fact. It does not create a general exception to the public-disclosure bar. 2023 published opinion.

The business takeaway is that public data can identify a lead, but a viable FCA case needs evidence that explains why specific claims were false and addresses plausible lawful explanations. The FCA's original-source test is different from the “original information” tests used in DOJ's CWA and WHIP. Qualifying under one program does not qualify someone under another.

Practical limits across programs

  • Lawsuits: FCA complaints begin under seal, but the relator's identity may become public later. The government can extend its review. If DOJ declines, the relator may have to fund and run the case against the defendant. A successful relator may recover fees and costs, but no law guarantees a lawyer will take the case or advance expenses. No program guarantees payment. No standard market fee was verified. The fee arrangement is negotiated. No attorney was contacted for this research.
  • DOJ tips: A named WHIP or CWA report does not require a lawyer. An anonymous report does. Anonymous reporting still requires DOJ to learn the person's identity before payment. DOJ may share information or disclose identity when required by law or in a proceeding.
  • Medicare: CMS needs contact information to pay a reward. Identity protection is subject to law. The cap and late payment trigger make this a small, delayed reward.
  • Multiple programs: WHIP says a person may qualify for another program, including CWA. CWA may reduce or deny overlapping awards and will not pay twice for the same recovery. CMS pays only when another law does not already provide a reward. FCA first-to-file and public-disclosure rules may independently block a later case. One evidence package may fit more than one program, but the rules do not guarantee multiple payments for the same recovery.

Practical conclusion

The completed Medicare/Medicaid and facility-screening prototype used free public CMS/HHS files. That experiment's no-purchase condition does not restrict future research. The current research scope includes government sources available by request and options costing less than $10,000. See government data access for access terms and limits. A narrow, code-specific Medicare FFS screen with provider identity, discipline, and exclusion checks remains the strongest near-term public-data path. Public records lack patient-level diagnoses, clinical notes, referral communications, and payer adjudication context. They can locate outliers, but cannot prove a false claim or establish reward eligibility. The new Medicaid source expands payer coverage, but should not be used for financial outlier scoring until its extraordinary payment values are reconciled. Public-record investigation and a legally compliant whistleblower submission are later steps. An anomaly score alone cannot produce them.

CMS Medicare Limited Data Set

Application and delivery

CMS calls its research products Limited Data Sets (LDS) and Research Identifiable Files (RIF). An LDS request requires a study application and Data Use Agreement (DUA). For new LDS DUAs effective August 11, 2026, the requesting organization must also have an approved Data Management Plan Self-Attestation Questionnaire (DMP SAQ) for the computer system where the data will be stored or processed.

Submit the request through CMS's Enterprise Privacy Policy Engine (EPPE). CMS makes the DUA with the organization, not the individual. The agreement cannot be transferred to another organization.

CMS delivers LDS files through the Chronic Conditions Warehouse (CCW) Secure File Transfer System (SFTS). Users need a CCW account, identity proofing, and security training. The files may be downloaded only to the computer system listed in the approved DMP SAQ and DUA. This differs from the RIF Innovator route, where users work in the VRDC and cannot download person-level records.

What the DUA permits

The August 2026 LDS DUA limits use to the research described in the request. It requires a minimum-necessary justification and public dissemination. Applicants must disclose planned commercial products. The DUA bars identifying or contacting beneficiaries, suppresses beneficiary cells of 1 to 10 in outputs, and requires prior written CMS permission to link the data to outside sources. It also prohibits publishing provider Tax Identification Numbers (TINs).

The DUA does not clearly address named-provider case reports, bounty or qui tam referrals, or sharing provider-level results with relator counsel. It also does not establish that those uses are authorized. Do not infer permission or a prohibition beyond the text. Ask CMS for a written answer about provider identities, outputs, external linkages, and any referrals before applying. Data may be retained for one year and then must be destroyed unless CMS approves an extension.

A crosswalk from National Provider Identifier (NPI) to exclusion, licensing, or court records is not authorized by default. A DUA that specifically describes the linkage and receives prior CMS approval may authorize that named linkage. The agreement clearly bars identifying or contacting beneficiaries. It does not state the same blanket prohibition for provider names.

Who is eligible

CMS accepts research requests from organizations and makes the organization responsible for the DUA and approved DMP SAQ. The current official materials reviewed do not say whether a solo researcher, sole proprietorship, or single-member LLC is categorically eligible or ineligible. They also do not impose a blanket ban on for-profit LDS requests. Applicants are expected to describe commercial products.

The project must be a systematic study for generalizable knowledge and results must be publicly disseminated. A project whose practical purpose is to select named providers for reward-seeking or legal action may not meet that test. CMS may approve or deny requests. The approved computing environment is a substantive requirement, not just paperwork.

Questions to resolve before applying

Ask CMS's DUA team in writing:

  • Can a U.S.-based, solo-owned LLC be the Requesting Organization for a one-year Carrier SAF study on a targeted procedure?
  • Is a publicly disseminated study of provider-level billing patterns allowed if it may also lead to referrals to OIG or relator counsel, or to a contingent recovery?
  • Will CMS authorize linkage to NPPES, LEIE, licensing, and court records?
  • Can a named-provider report leave the approved environment or be shared with government enforcement or counsel?
  • What is the current all-in invoice for the exact file, year, and tier?

The official LDS process page lists DataUseAgreement@cms.hhs.gov for questions. This review did not send a message.

California HPD and DHCS Medi-Cal data

What HPD contains

California's Health Care Payments Database (HPD) is the state All-Payer Claims Database (APCD). HCAI says it includes claims or encounters, enrollment, provider, and pharmacy data from commercial coverage, Medicare Advantage, Medi-Cal, and some other populations. Current requestable claims cover 2018 to 2024. Newer years generally arrive about 15 months after the service year. The data include procedures, diagnoses, charges, and paid amounts, but not clinical notes.

Medicare Fee-for-Service data are available only to state agencies. Medi-Cal data require DHCS approval.

Access levels and provider identifiers

  • Standard Limited is a record-level dataset.
  • Standard Limited Plus adds direct identifiers for providers and health plans. It is available only in HCAI's Secure Data Enclave.
  • Custom Limited removes identifiers. It may be used in the enclave or approved for direct transmission.
  • Research Identifiable includes direct patient identifiers and requires a recommendation from HCAI's Data Release Committee (DRC).

The enclave includes SQL, Python, and R tools, controlled uploads, analysis, and output review. Users cannot freely extract raw data. HCAI reviews and approves outputs. Provider identities may be visible inside the enclave with Standard Limited Plus. Whether a named-provider report can be exported or shared for bounty or legal purposes needs confirmation from HCAI.

Price and application steps

HCAI says an individual or organization may apply. Register in its Data Request Portal, provide the request and required documents, pay the $100 application fee, complete review, and sign the data request and DUA. If approved, HCAI credits the application fee toward the data charge. If denied, the fee is not refundable. HCAI targets 120 days for review, but does not guarantee a decision by then.

The Standard Limited or Standard Limited Plus enclave price is $5,000 per project space per year plus $5,000 per year for one Analyst seat. A Researcher seat costs $6,000 per year. The base project and Analyst price is exactly $10,000 per year. It is outside the strict under-$10,000 budget at list price. Optional software and storage may cost extra.

For comparison, direct transmission of Standard Limited is $20,000 per year for commercial records or $22,000 for commercial records plus Medi-Cal. Custom Limited or Research Identifiable direct transfer adds a $9,000 custom dataset charge plus per-year table fees. A commercial bounty project is not clearly eligible for a discount. Do not assume a price reduction.

Medi-Cal use limits

HCAI says a request that includes Medi-Cal needs DHCS approval. DHCS's separate protected-data process accepts research and public-health purposes, not legal or administrative purposes. DHCS says its DRC generally will not support research leading to a product the researcher or funder intends to market.

This creates a strong mismatch for a commercial bounty product or lead-generation pipeline. It does not prove that all Medi-Cal use is impossible. HCAI reviews project-specific requests, and DHCS's rule applies to protected-data requests. Before paying the application fee, ask HCAI and DHCS whether the proposed purpose, provider identifiers, outputs, and external referrals are allowed.

What the CMS Carrier 5% sample can show

The 5% Carrier SAF samples beneficiaries, not providers or service lines. It retains sampled claim lines. A low-volume provider may have no sampled patients or too few observations to support a stable rate. This limits rare-event detection.

Carrier claim lines include performing and billing NPI, HCPCS, service dates, allowed charges, and Medicare payments. Check the 2024 onward data dictionary for exact fields. The annual 5% file can help investigate a provider or procedure already identified from another source, but it is not a full census.

The free Physician and Other Practitioners file covers a broader Medicare Fee-for-Service provider population, subject to suppression for low beneficiary counts, but has no claim lines. Carrier data have more detail but sampling gaps. Use the public file for broad screening and Carrier as a follow-up source, subject to CMS approval for the use.

Sources: CMS Carrier SAF page and current dictionaries, ResDAC Carrier file description, and CMS physician public data.

FOIA and California Public Records Act

The federal Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) and California Public Records Act (CPRA) let any person request existing, nonexempt records. The requester does not need a special credential. CMS FOIA accepts requests for agency records, including provider records, investigations, cost reports, and policy documents. The Medicare beneficiary-claims FOIA portal requires beneficiary identity or authorization. It is not a route to arbitrary people's claims. CMS directs people seeking Medicaid claims to the state Medicaid agency. That does not establish that CMS has no national Medicaid research files, such as TAF or RIF products available through separate research channels.

California DHCS accepts public-records requests through its website, in writing, by phone, or in person. Confidential records are not available through that route. A requester can ask for existing aggregate tables, published reports, audit summaries, or already-redacted records. Specify the agency, date range, HCPCS or procedure, payer or program, aggregation level, and known privacy suppression rules.

Do not ask the agency to “run a fraud analysis” or request member-level claims. Agencies need not answer questions, create a new report, or release protected claims. Fees may cover copies or programming. CMS may also charge allowed processing costs. Set a clear fee limit in the request.

Low-cost request to try first

Ask CMS FOIA for existing public-release aggregate or provider-level tables, reports, and statistical analyses for the named procedure codes and years. Also request existing records explaining definitions and suppression fields. Ask DHCS CPRA for existing redacted aggregate reports or audits for Medi-Cal by target code and service year, with cells suppressed where needed.

A denial or a response that no existing record is available does not show that fraud did not occur. It only shows what the agency can provide through that public-records route.

California HIE and TEFCA

California's Data Exchange Framework (DxF) and the federal Trusted Exchange Framework and Common Agreement (TEFCA) support information sharing among healthcare and public-health organizations, payers, and patients under governance agreements. They do not provide a public database for a private investigator.

TEFCA requires a stated Exchange Purpose. Current purposes include treatment, payment, healthcare operations, public health, government-benefits determination, and individual access services. An individual-access service retrieves a person's own records at that person's request or consent. It is not a population claims feed. California's DxF Data Sharing Agreement governs secure sharing among signatories under applicable privacy laws and purpose restrictions. It does not create an independent right to search records for bounty leads.

A private entity might participate as a technology provider or subparticipant through an existing network. This requires a contract, identity and security controls, and a permitted purpose. No official source reviewed provides a fee schedule or a general investigator eligibility path. No independent authorized access route for this project has been established. A legitimate payer or covered-entity arrangement could change feasibility, but none is established.

Reading the amounts correctly

A settlement amount is what a defendant agreed to pay to resolve specified claims. It can include government restitution, civil penalties, state and federal shares, fees, costs, and relator awards. A settlement total is not automatically the amount of claims billed, the amount paid on those claims, or a final finding of fraud. In the examples above, only the primary record's specific share term or allocation is counted as the relator's potential award. Where the record says “will receive” or makes payment conditional, the amount is not treated as cash already received.

Case records and source detail

Two healthcare fraud prosecutions: what public records show

Research cut-off: 25 October 2026. Both cases are pending criminal prosecutions. An indictment records allegations made by a grand jury. It is not a finding of guilt. The defendants are presumed innocent unless proven guilty. The dollar amounts below are allegations, not established losses, restitution, forfeiture, or money recovered.

1. Atherectomy case: United States v. Harold Tabaie and Hany Malek

Who is charged and where the case stands

Harold A. Tabaie is a cardiothoracic surgeon. Hany Malek is a chiropractor. The indictment says they co-owned and operated Angelus Medical Clinic Multi Specialty Group and West Regional Cardio Thoracic and Vascular Surgeons, also known as West Regional Vein Center.

The CMS National Provider Identifier Registry lists Tabaie under individual NPI 1346286663. NPI means National Provider Identifier, a number used to identify a healthcare provider in billing and enrollment records. CMS's 2026 Medicare Revalidation List links that individual NPI to group NPI 1528740024, Southern California Cardiothoracic and Vascular Surgeons. These identifiers help connect public records. The indictment does not list NPIs.

The case is in the Central District of California, case 2:26-cr-00668-JFW, before Judge John F. Walter. A grand jury indictment was filed on 20 October 2026 and announced on 22 October. The DOJ case page lists four healthcare fraud counts against Tabaie and Malek, plus two bank fraud counts against Malek. The case was pending with no disposition listed as of 25 October 2026.

The free primary charging record is the DOJ-hosted 17-page indictment. I did not locate a separate public criminal complaint or affidavit.

What prosecutors allege

The indictment alleges a scheme from January 2016 through August 2023 to defraud Medicare through the two clinics. It says Malek met patients, reviewed tests, told patients they needed surgery, and referred them to Tabaie, although Malek was not a medical doctor. Prosecutors allege that Tabaie performed atherectomies on arteries without disease, repeated procedures after prior failures, and used procedures when less invasive options were appropriate.

The indictment also alleges that the defendants offered kickbacks such as transportation, groceries, household goods, utility payments, prescription costs, and $50 to $100 gift cards. It alleges they steered patients into Medicare plans that reimbursed the clinics more, billed some claims under other doctors' names, and split services into separate claims to increase payment. These are allegations, not established facts.

Prosecutors allege that the clinics billed Medicare about $134,955,000 and received at least about $54,817,000. Four illustrative fraud counts describe claims of about $14,000 each, submitted on 29 September 2021, 3 August 2022, 22 November 2022, and 14 September 2023. The indictment also seeks criminal forfeiture if the defendants are convicted. It does not establish a final loss or forfeiture amount.

How the case was detected

The DOJ's 22 October Central District announcement says data analysis identified Tabaie as an extreme national outlier in Medicare atherectomy billing. The separate DOJ case-status page does not name a detection method. The indictment also does not explain how investigators first found the case or identify their data source.

What an outside analyst can see

CMS publishes a free Medicare Physician & Other Practitioners file, organized by provider, procedure code, and place of service. It summarizes Original Medicare Part B claims, including service volume, submitted charges, and payments. NPI identifies the provider. HCPCS is the procedure or service code used in billing. Place of service identifies the setting. The file reports grouped totals, not individual claims.

A researcher can use it to look for unusually high provider volume and compare providers in the same specialty, year, and setting. That can produce a lead similar to the outlier signal DOJ described. It cannot establish whether a procedure was medically necessary, who actually performed it, what a patient's condition was, whether a claim was false, or what the defendant knew. The file covers Original Medicare fee-for-service, not commercial insurance or all Medicare Advantage claims. It does not include patient records, imaging, operative notes, referrals, complications, or intent. Small beneficiary counts are suppressed. The indictment also alleges that some claims used other doctors' names, which limits a screen that looks only at Tabaie's NPI.

It does not prove these were the codes on the charged claims or that any service was improper.

2. Substance-treatment and sober-home case: United States v. Nathan Young et al.

Who is charged and where the case stands

The principal indictment names Nathan Young, also known as “Pablo,” David Young, also known as “Sancho Lopez,” Honey Phipps, and Cody Phipps. It alleges that Nathan and his father David controlled dozens of facilities, including Rodeo Recovery LLC. It alleges that Honey and Cody Phipps worked as patient brokers and sometimes acted as nominee owners or operators. Rodeo Recovery is one company in the alleged network. The prosecution is not a case against Rodeo alone.

The case is in the Central District of California, case 2:26-cr-00670-PA, before Judge Percy Anderson. The free public copy of the indictment is stamped filed 21 October 2026. DOJ announced the charges on 22 October. The prosecution remained pending as of 25 October. An arrest, indictment, bail condition, or forfeiture allegation is not a conviction or a recovered loss.

The free 42-page indictment is a court charging document hosted by Courthouse News. The document identifies the court case and filing date. DOJ's takedown announcement summarizes the charges but does not link the indictment. I did not locate a separate public complaint affidavit.

What prosecutors allege

The indictment alleges a scheme from May 2020 through May 2026 in Los Angeles County and elsewhere. It involves private health plans, including Anthem, Aetna, Cigna, and UnitedHealthcare. Prosecutors allege that patient brokers recruited vulnerable people with offers of money, drugs, flights, and housing. They allege that patients were enrolled in better-paying insurance plans even when they were not eligible, then billed for treatment that was unnecessary, deficient, or never provided. The services named include detox, residential treatment, partial hospitalization, intensive outpatient, and outpatient care.

The indictment also alleges that some patients were coached to fake detox symptoms, that large Zoom sessions billed as therapy sometimes provided no therapy, and that some patients were drugged, isolated, or physically prevented from leaving. These are allegations and have not been adjudicated.

Prosecutors allege that the network submitted $1,619,276,243 in claims and received at least $210,274,793 from insurers. Neither amount is a court-established loss, damages award, restitution, or collected forfeiture. The indictment includes three forfeiture allegations, including a San Diego residence. Those requests apply only if the relevant defendants are convicted. The indictment does not say that $1.6 billion or $210 million was recovered.

DOJ separately announced that Nicholas Moore, an alleged area manager in a related case, pleaded guilty and admitted assisting with $199 million in claims. Moore's plea is evidence in his case. It is not a conviction of the four Young and Phipps defendants.

How the case was detected

DOJ lists the FBI, IRS Criminal Investigation, and DEA as investigating agencies. The takedown announcement includes a general statement from IRS-CI about uncovering complex fraud by following the money. It does not say what triggered this specific case or whether an insurer, public complaint, journalist, or whistleblower brought it to investigators. The indictment describes evidence prosecutors say they gathered, but not how the investigation began.

What an outside analyst can see

Before the federal indictment, public records included a 2020 nuisance lawsuit by the City of Beverly Hills involving Rodeo Recovery and Nathan Young. The defendants denied the nuisance allegations in their answer. Aetna filed a 2023 federal civil insurance-fraud case involving Young-affiliated companies. Its 2024 amended complaint added Aetna's allegations. Local and national reporting also discussed the industry and, by 2025, a federal investigation.

These records show that allegations and warning signs were public before the indictment. They do not show that federal investigators relied on them, that the allegations were true, or that the civil plaintiffs shared evidence with law enforcement.

An outside researcher can review business and licensing records, civil filings, and news reports. But private insurers do not publish provider-by-procedure claims files like CMS's Original Medicare file. The main questions in this case concern insurance eligibility, payments, actual treatment, attendance, medical necessity, patient identity, broker payments, and money flows. Those depend on private claims, patient records, messages, and financial records. Facility names, patient complaints, news reports, or an insurer's lawsuit are not enough to conclude that a claim was false.

Any whistleblower or reward?

The indictment, DOJ announcement, and other public records reviewed do not identify a whistleblower who brought the federal case or an award recipient. The prior Aetna and Beverly Hills cases do not prove that a whistleblower started the federal investigation. The source of the case and any reward remain unknown from the public record reviewed.

What these two cases mean for an outside researcher

The atherectomy case is the more reproducible starting point because DOJ confirms that data analysis found an outlier and CMS publishes relevant Medicare billing summaries. A public screen can flag unusual volume. It cannot establish medical necessity, correct provider identity, intent, or loss.

The substance-treatment case is harder to investigate from public claims data because the alleged payments came from commercial insurers. Public lawsuits and reports existed before the indictment, but their role in the federal investigation is unknown. The core claims and treatment evidence are private.

In either case, a public lead discovered after charges cannot be treated as a new tip. Billed charges and payer payments are different amounts. Neither case's public records reviewed here identify an outsider receiving a reward.

Outside-analyst court and recovery precedents

What outside-data healthcare FCA cases show

Research date: 25 October 2026. This review asks whether public healthcare data can lead to a payment for an outside analyst. It summarizes court and government records. It is not legal advice or a forecast of success.

Short answer

Some data analytics firms have received DOJ-announced shares of settlements. The public records do not show that these firms succeeded using public data alone. In two hospital cases, Integra Med Analytics relied mainly on statistical comparisons of Medicare claims. Courts dismissed both cases at the pleading stage because the statistics did not sufficiently distinguish fraud from lawful explanations. Those dismissals did not decide that no fraud occurred.

A separate case against a vascular practice and physician resulted in an announced settlement share of about $976,000 for Lincoln Analytics. The DOJ release does not describe the firm's methods or data sources. Another Integra case led to an announced $45,000 share on a $300,000 settlement based on the defendants' ability to pay. The releases say the relators will receive these shares. No bank receipts were independently verified here.

Fixed definitions and source selection

  • We used the 2021, 2022 and 2023 files because they overlap DOJ's alleged conduct period, January 2016 to August 2023. The full 2023 file includes months after the alleged period. The indictment identifies a claim submitted in September 2023, but annual files cannot isolate the relevant months.
  • Primary lower-extremity atherectomy codes: 37225, 37227, 37229, 37231. Additional-vessel codes 37233, 37235 are retrieved but excluded from primary totals. Supra-inguinal codes 0234T to 0238T are outside this bounded lower-extremity screen. This is not a complete recreation of the government's scope or all atherectomy billing.
  • Code meanings are supported by observed historical CMS code rows, the CMS coding manual and the contemporaneous 2023 Kentucky Medicaid physician fee schedule. Codes were available within the selected years. Code-specific totals remain separate. No claim is made about compatibility with changed codes in later years.
  • Source API versions are pinned by UUID in run_analysis.py and catalog-snapshot.json. Historical releases can be revised. manifest.json records exact URLs, retrieval timestamps, hashes, and row counts for the saved snapshots.
  • Comparison population: U.S. individual providers (Rndrng_Prvdr_Ent_Cd=I), shown separately for facility (F) and non-facility (O) settings. Tabaie's observed rows are O. This is CMS's aggregate non-facility category, not a detailed location code for a particular clinic.
  • National primary-volume rank sums service units across the four primary codes for each rendering NPI, year, and setting. The same-specialty rank compares only providers in the CMS specialty listed for that year. Rank equals one plus the number of providers with a strictly larger total. Ties share a rank.
  • Code-specific volume and services-per-beneficiary ranks compare the same code, year, and setting. Specialty-adjusted volume ranks are also reported. There is no clinical risk adjustment: public procedure rows lack the patient-level severity and diagnostic detail necessary for that.
  • Estimated payment equals Tot_Srvcs × Avg_Mdcr_Pymt_Amt, summed only over the selected code rows. It is a reconstruction from rounded averages, not a claim-by-claim audit, fraud-loss estimate, or the indictment's total.
  • Never add beneficiary counts across codes: the same patient can appear in multiple rows. Primary service units are also not unique surgeries, patients, limbs, or encounters.

What was exploratory

This case was already known, so selection has hindsight bias. The codes and 2021 to 2023 year range were fixed before the full national extraction and ranking. A target-NPI lookup and one-code access probe had already shown two relevant codes before the scripted experiment. This was not preregistered or blinded. No rank threshold was tuned to obtain a particular answer. The top-20 review uses the latest selected year (2023), non-facility setting, the four primary codes, and descending units, breaking display-order ties by NPI. It does not train a model or estimate sensitivity, specificity, precision, prevalence, or a payout probability.

Top-20 review and lawful alternative explanations

top20-review.csv and top20-review-notes.md examine the twenty highest-volume 2023 non-facility results. The review export uses anonymous result labels except that it identifies which label is the already-public historical case. Raw public-source files and full descriptive rank tables retain original provider names/NPIs for reproducibility. Their inclusion is not an allegation against those providers.

The review is desk triage of aggregate data, not twenty clinical or investigative case reviews. It considers specialty concentration, unusually small peer groups, repeated-service ratios, multi-vessel coding, setting differences, referral concentration, and absent severity information. All twenty remain unadjudicated by this analysis. We obtained no records, interviews, patient authorizations, or expert opinions.

Timing and leakage

The current catalog marks the retrieved 2021/2022 versions modified in March 2025 and points to replacement files under November 2025 paths. The 2023 entry is modified October 28, 2025. These fields do not establish initial release dates. A CMS bulletin dated May 22, 2025 independently confirms publication of CY2023 physician data by that date, before the October 2026 prosecution. The current snapshot is not necessarily byte-for-byte the initial 2025 release.

We have not established that these exact versions were public before August 2023 or before the government began investigating. A retrospective rank is therefore not proof of early discovery or reward originality. The 2026 NPPES identity check and indictment were used for validation after case selection. They cannot be treated as newly available prospective evidence.
